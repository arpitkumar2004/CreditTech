Executive Summary

Why alternative credit scoring, and why now

India's formal financial system has won the battle for accounts but not yet the battle for credit. Account ownership among Indian adults reached 89% in 2024, up from 35% in 2011 (Klapper et al., 2025), yet the share of adults who borrowed formally in the year prior to the 2021 Findex survey was only 13% — ten points below the developing-economy average, and lower still for women (World Bank, 2023). Traditional bureau-based scoring depends on a repayment history that thin-file farmers, self-help group (SHG) members, and micro-enterprises largely do not have: 57% of rural households are agricultural (NABARD, 2024), average landholding has shrunk to 0.74 hectares (NABARD, 2024), and the MSME sector alone carries an addressable credit gap of roughly ₹30 lakh crore, or 24% of total debt demand (SIDBI, 2025). CreditTech proposes a village-first, consent-based, multi-source alternative credit scoring system that layers transaction, geospatial, agronomic, SHG-group, and utility-payment data on top of India's public digital infrastructure (Aadhaar e-KYC, UPI, Account Aggregator, and the RBI's Unified Lending Interface) to make previously invisible borrowers legible to formal lenders — without displacing the institutions (NABARD, cooperative banks, MFIs, SHG federations) that already carry rural trust.

Problem & Village-Level Need Assessment

Secondary-data synthesis of the credit-access gap facing farmers, SHGs, and rural micro-enterprises, and why that gap is structurally a data problem rather than only a capital problem.

1.1 The inclusion paradox: accounts without credit

Two decades of push — Jan Dhan, Aadhaar, and UPI (the "JAM trinity") — have closed India's account-ownership gap almost completely. But an account is not a credit history. The RBI's own Financial Inclusion Index (FI-Index), which weights usage at 45% and access at only 35%, rose from 43.4 in March 2017 to just 67.0 in March 2025 (RBI, 2025) — meaningful progress, but still a third of the way short of full inclusion, and driven disproportionately by payments usage rather than credit usage. The underlying asymmetry is stark: 43% of PMJDY accounts remain inactive (Duvendack et al., 2023, as cited in The India Forum, 2025), and in the 2021 Findex round only 13% of Indian adults had borrowed from a formal source in the preceding year — compared with 23% across developing economies generally, and just 10% among Indian women (World Bank, 2023).

1.2 Why bureau-based scoring fails this population

Conventional bureau scores (e.g., CIBIL/TransUnion) are built on repayment trails from formal credit accounts. They structurally under-score three groups that together define CreditTech's target population:

Farmers and allied-activity households

57% of rural households are classified agricultural, up from 48% in 2016–17 (NABARD, 2024), yet farm income is seasonal, weather-exposed, and rarely documented in a bank-legible form. Institutional-loan uptake among agricultural households did rise from 60.5% to 75.5% between 2016–17 and 2021–22 (NABARD, 2024) — largely via the Kisan Credit Card — but 23.4% still borrow only from non-institutional (often high-cost) sources, and agri-loans carry the highest sectoral GNPA ratio in the banking system (RBI, 2024), which makes banks structurally risk-averse toward exactly this segment.

SHG members and rural micro-enterprises

SHG-Bank Linkage is the world's largest microcredit programme, with cumulative bank credit to SHGs exceeding ₹12.18 lakh crore since 2013–14 and repayment discipline above 96% (PIB, 2025; Anantam IAS, 2026) — a track record that traditional bureau scoring barely captures, because SHG internal thrift and peer-guarantee behaviour never touches a credit bureau. Meanwhile 12% of India's micro-enterprises (as opposed to 2–3% of small/medium ones) still borrow informally, and the MSME credit gap widens to 35% for women-owned enterprises (SIDBI, 2025).

1.3 The connectivity floor is now high enough to build on

The technical precondition for alternative, mobile-first scoring — rural connectivity — has crossed a threshold in the last three years. Rural India now hosts roughly 548 million active internet users out of 958 million nationally, and is growing at nearly four times the pace of urban India (IAMAI–Kantar, Internet in India Report 2025). This closes the "digital divide" objection that stalled earlier alternative-scoring proposals and makes app-based, low-bandwidth data capture (rather than only assisted/BC-channel capture) newly viable at village scale.

The core problem statement

India has built world-class rails for payments and identity but not yet for creditworthiness signals at the base of the pyramid. The result is a persistent mismatch: institutions such as NABARD, SHG-federations, and the KCC scheme have proven that rural repayment discipline is high, but underwriting technology has not caught up to price and extend that trust efficiently, especially for new-to-credit borrowers, women, and small/marginal farmers below the KCC/collateral threshold.

Institutional design principle

CreditTech is deliberately architected as a decision-support and scoring layer that sits between borrower-side data sources and lender-side underwriting systems — consistent with how the Account Aggregator ecosystem and OCEN are designed to "unbundle" lending into specialised roles (loan service provider, technology service provider, and regulated lender) rather than create a new deposit-taking or lending entity (Sahamati, 2025; ocen.dev, 2025). This keeps CreditTech within RBI's Lending Service Provider (LSP) framework under the 2022/2025 Digital Lending Directions, avoiding the regulatory burden of becoming a Regulated Entity itself.

03

Literature, Policy & Real-World Case Evidence

This section is deliberately the most detailed in the report. It synthesises (a) the global academic literature on alternative and AI-driven credit scoring, (b) India-specific empirical and policy literature, (c) technical literature on fairness, bias, and explainability, and (d) real-world fintech case evidence — before distilling a research gap that justifies CreditTech's design choices.

3.1 Global academic literature on alternative data credit scoring

The foundational premise of alternative credit scoring — that non-traditional data can substitute for a thin or absent credit file — is now well established across development-finance and machine-learning literature. Berg et al. (2020) demonstrated in the Review of Financial Studies that "digital footprint" variables (device type, email domain, time-of-day of application) predict default comparably to, and sometimes better than, traditional bureau scores for e-commerce borrowers, establishing the empirical basis that behavioural and device-level metadata carry real credit signal even where no repayment history exists. A 2021 Risks journal review of fintech and financial inclusion catalogued this shift across markets, concluding that digital-footprint and mobile-transaction-based scoring systematically raises approval rates for populations excluded by bureau-only models (as cited in Jonnalagadda & Babu, 2026).

Systematic review

Machine Learning Technologies for Digital Credit Scoring in Rural Finance: A Literature Review — this MDPI Risks paper systematically reviews AI/ML-based credit-scoring methods adopted by financial institutions globally for rural and smallholder lending, finding that ML-based scoring consistently reduces human bias in loan decisions relative to manual underwriting, but that adoption is gated by institutional readiness rather than by model performance.

Kshetri, N. (2021). Machine learning (ML) technologies for digital credit scoring in rural finance: A literature review. Risks, 9(11), 192.
Systematic review · 2025

Revolutionizing agricultural loan recommendation systems via machine learning and artificial intelligence — a 2025 ScienceDirect systematic review of the full ML pipeline applied to agricultural credit (input-variable selection, data acquisition, model building, and deployment) finds that most prior reviews omit one or more pipeline stages and that very few studies address interpretability or data-availability challenges specific to smallholders — a gap this report treats as central to CreditTech's design (see §3.5).

Systematic review authors (2025). Revolutionizing agricultural loan recommendation systems via machine learning and artificial intelligence: A systematic literature review. Computers and Electronics in Agriculture.
Applied ML · Kenya

Towards Small-Scale Farmers' Fair Credit Scoring Technique — using field data from smallholder farmers in Kakamega County, Kenya, this study models credit scoring as a "bandit-with-knapsack" exploration problem, combining a Fuzzy Unordered Rule Induction Algorithm with a Fuzzy Analytical Hierarchy Process to rank scoring rules under severe data sparsity — directly evidencing that specialised algorithms, not merely "more data," are required to score sparse smallholder profiles fairly.

Njuguna, C., & Sowon, K. (2020). Towards small-scale farmers' fair credit scoring technique. Proceedings of the 2nd African Conference on Human Computer Interaction.
Practitioner evidence

CGAP × Harvesting × PRIDE Microfinance (Uganda) — CGAP's applied research with agri-fintech Harvesting and Uganda's largest MFI built a credit-scoring model blending traditional MFI lending data with alternative sources (satellite imagery, value-chain transaction data, mobile network operator records) for coffee-farmer lending, concluding that understanding a smallholder's agricultural activity — not only their financial activity — is the precondition for predicting cash flow and, therefore, repayment capacity.

CGAP. (2018). CGAP and Harvesting explore use of alternative data in credit scores [Blog post]. Consultative Group to Assist the Poor, World Bank.
FAO / STI Portal

Alternative Data Credit Scoring Platforms for Smallholder Farmers — FAO's Science, Technology and Innovation Portal defines the category formally: platforms that combine mobile-recorded farm and financial activity with backend analytics processing satellite imagery, agronomic, and mobile-money data to generate credit-risk profiles without requiring a conventional credit history or collateral, citing multi-country deployments (Brazil, India, Kenya, Myanmar, Uganda, Zambia) of the Harvesting Credit Risk Scoring tool as an early reference implementation.

Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations. (2020). Alternative data credit scoring platforms for smallholder farmers. FAO STI Portal.

3.2 India-specific empirical and policy literature

India-specific literature is smaller but rapidly growing, catalysed by the maturation of the Account Aggregator (AA) framework and the RBI's digital-lending regulatory response. Three strands are relevant to CreditTech's design.

India · Empirical · 2026

Drivers of Credit Uptake by Smallholder Farmers: Empirical Evidence from India's Agricultural Sector — using primary survey data from 96 smallholder farmers, this study maps both traditional and alternative data points onto the classical "5 Cs of credit" (character, capacity, capital, collateral, conditions) to build a weighted alternative credit-scoring model, offering one of the few India-specific empirical validations that alternative indicators (asset ownership, cropping pattern, group affiliation) meaningfully explain formal-credit uptake alongside conventional variables.

Jonnalagadda, A. K., & Babu, S. R. (2026). Drivers of credit uptake by smallholder farmers: Empirical evidence from India's agricultural sector. International Research Journal of Multidisciplinary Scope, 7(2), 110–119.
India · DPI ecosystem study · 2025

The Transformative Impact of the Account Aggregator Framework on Financial Inclusion in India — this multi-sectoral study of MSME, microfinance, and personal lending documents that the AA ecosystem grew from launch (September 2021) to more than 100 million successful consents by mid-2024, with roughly 80–90 million unique users, and that AA-sourced transaction-history analysis has cut loan-decisioning time from days to minutes for "new-to-credit" or thin-file borrowers — directly evidencing the DPI foundation CreditTech is designed to sit on.

Advances in Consumer Research. (2025). The transformative impact of the Account Aggregator framework on financial inclusion in India: A multi-sectoral study of MSMEs, microfinance, and personal lending. Advances in Consumer Research.
India · NABARD primary survey

NABARD All-India Rural Financial Inclusion Survey (NAFIS) 2021–22 — the largest primary dataset on rural financial behaviour in India (1 lakh households, 28 states, 10,000 village blocks), NAFIS is the report's single most important empirical anchor for the problem statement: it evidences rising institutional-credit uptake (60.5%→75.5% among agricultural households) alongside a still-substantial 23.4% reliance on non-institutional sources, alongside gains in savings, insurance, and financial literacy.

National Bank for Agriculture and Rural Development. (2024). All India Rural Financial Inclusion Survey (NAFIS) 2021–22. NABARD, Department of Economic Analysis and Research.

3.3 Fairness, explainability, and algorithmic bias — the technical governance literature

Because CreditTech's mandate is explicitly inclusion, the report treats the fairness/explainability literature as core rather than peripheral — an AI model that reduces exclusion for farmers while introducing new discrimination against women or low-literacy borrowers would defeat its own purpose.

Systematic review · 43 studies, 2020–2025

Performance, Fairness, and Explainability in AI-Based Credit Scoring — synthesising 43 peer-reviewed studies (2020–2025) from IEEE Xplore, Scopus, Web of Science, and Science Direct, this review finds explainability was the fastest-growing research pillar between 2023 and 2024, reflecting tightening regulatory expectations, and confirms there is still no universal, jurisdiction-independent fairness definition — a finding CreditTech treats as a design constraint (§13) rather than a solved problem.

Systematic review authors. (2026). Performance, fairness, and explainability in AI-based credit scoring: A systematic literature review. Risks, 19(2), 104.
Governance review

Algorithmic Bias and Fairness in AI Credit Scoring: Evidence, Mechanisms, and Governance Responses — reviewing 15 empirical studies from a corpus of 55 peer-reviewed papers (2018–2025), this paper concludes that bias in AI credit scoring is not primarily a technical failure: it originates in historically generated training data, is amplified through correlated proxy variables (e.g., geography or device type standing in for gender or caste), and persists because institutional incentives do not penalise discriminatory outcomes absent regulatory compulsion — directly motivating CreditTech's proposed independent fairness-audit function (§13).

Journal of Management Science Research Review. (2026). Algorithmic bias and fairness in AI credit scoring: Evidence, mechanisms, and governance responses. Journal of Management Science Research Review.
Trade-off framework

Bücker et al. (2020) and Agosto et al. (2023) — foundational work establishing the accuracy–fairness–explainability trade-off: interpretable models built for regulatory compliance accept higher reserves or more credit defaults as the price of transparency, while Agosto et al.'s Sustainability–Accuracy–Fairness–Explainability (SAFE) framework offers a structured way to evaluate AI credit-scoring systems across all four dimensions simultaneously rather than optimising any one in isolation.

Bücker, M., Szepannek, G., Gosiewska, A., & Biecek, P. (2020). Transparency, auditability, and explainability of machine learning models in credit scoring. Journal of the Operational Research Society. · Agosto, A., Giudici, P., & Raffinetti, E. (2023). A sustainable, accurate, fair and explainable (SAFE) artificial intelligence model. Data Science, 2(2), 197–217.

3.4 Real-world case evidence: fintechs already operating this model

CreditTech's architecture is not speculative — each of its core components has an operating precedent in the Indian or global market. The four cases below are the closest structural analogues.

India · Alternative-data lending infrastructure

Kaleidofin (Ki Score / Ki View)

A Chennai-based B2B2C risk-infrastructure provider founded in 2017, Kaleidofin's "Ki Score" combines geospatial socio-economic and climate-resilience data with customer contextual and behavioural data — trained on tens of millions of data points — to underwrite borrowers with no conventional credit history. As of late 2025 the platform had credit-decisioned close to 10 million customers (98% women) and enabled roughly USD 8 billion in disbursements across more than 57 partner MFIs, NBFCs, and banks, reporting 20–30% higher approval rates than bureau-only models with portfolio-at-risk (PAR90) held below 2%.

Kaleidofin Private Limited (2025); Munjuluri, R. (2025). Comparative analysis of alternative credit scoring models: Expanding access and identifying limitations [SSRN Working Paper].
India · Satellite / earth-observation agri-lending

SatSure (SatSource / SatCollect)

Founded in Bengaluru (2017) by former ISRO scientists, SatSure builds "digital twins" of farm plots from satellite imagery (13 spectral bands, five-day revisit), reaching 90–95% crop-identification accuracy despite India's fragmented landholding pattern, and layering socio-economic indicators (road density, market access, rail connectivity) on top. Its bank partnerships (including with TransUnion CIBIL and major commercial banks) have cut agricultural loan turnaround from 10–15 days to under 30 minutes, and its 2025 state-wide Uttar Pradesh rollout alone covers a state holding 17–18% of India's active KCC accounts.

SatSure Analytics (2025); The Better India (2026). How satellite data is helping farmers access faster agricultural loans in India.
Global comparative study

Comparative evidence: Upstart, Tala, Kaleidofin

A 2025 SSRN comparative study of Upstart (US), Tala (emerging markets, mobile-first), and Kaleidofin (India) finds that AI-driven alternative scoring approves 20–44% more borrowers than traditional bureau models across all three, with Tala reporting 63% of its 7.5 million-plus borrowers as first-time digital borrowers and 84% reporting improved quality of life — while flagging data privacy, algorithmic bias, and regulatory uncertainty as the consistent limiting factors across all three markets, precisely the risks CreditTech's governance framework (§13) is built to pre-empt.

Munjuluri, R. (2025). Comparative analysis of alternative credit scoring models: Expanding access and identifying limitations. SSRN.
India · Public digital infrastructure

RBI's Unified Lending Interface (ULI)

Evolving from a September 2022 pilot for digitising sub-₹1.6 lakh KCC loans, then the "Public Tech Platform for Frictionless Credit" (August 2023), and formally rebranded ULI at the RBI@90 conference (August 2024), the platform is an explicit "UPI-for-lending" analogue: a universal API gateway giving consenting lenders access to land records, satellite data, and dairy/milk-federation data alongside AA-sourced financial data. As of December 2024 it had disbursed over 600,000 loans worth ₹27,000 crore, though bankers report scale-up remains constrained by slow land-record digitisation and cautious adoption by larger lenders — a real-world constraint CreditTech's DPI integration plan (§9) explicitly accounts for.

Reserve Bank of India (2024, 2025); Business Standard (2026). Muted response from large lenders hinders ULI scale-up.

Stated research gap

The literature strongly supports the technical feasibility of alternative credit scoring and documents its impact at national/portfolio scale for individual fintechs. What it does not yet provide is a replicable, multi-institution, village-level design that integrates simultaneously with India's public DPI stack (AA + ULI), community institutions (SHGs/FPOs), and a formal fairness-audit process, validated across more than one agro-climatic zone. CreditTech's pilot (§13) is scoped to close exactly this gap.

Complete System Architecture

A five-layer architecture — identity & consent, data ingestion, intelligence, decisioning, and channel — designed so that no single vendor or data rail is a single point of failure.

Design principle: graceful degradation

Because ULI adoption by large lenders remains uneven and satellite coverage or SHG digitisation varies by district (see §3.4), the scoring engine is designed to produce a confidence-weighted score from whichever data sources are actually available for a given borrower — rather than requiring all five ingestion feeds to be present, which would simply recreate exclusion for the least-digitised villages.

What CreditTech deliberately does not ingest

Consistent with the fairness literature in §3.3 and DPDP Act's data-minimisation principle, the design explicitly excludes caste, religion, and other protected-attribute proxies from the feature set, and treats geography-as-proxy-for-social-category as a monitored bias risk rather than a usable feature (per the proxy-variable finding in the algorithmic-bias literature, §3.3).

Security, Privacy & Regulatory Framework

CreditTech's licence to operate rests on three overlapping regimes — RBI's digital-lending directions, the DPDP Act/Rules, and the Account Aggregator master directions — plus a self-imposed fairness-governance layer the literature (§3.3) shows regulation alone does not yet guarantee.

12.1 Regulatory framework

Table 12.1 — Applicable regulatory instruments
InstrumentKey requirement for CreditTech
RBI Digital Lending Directions (Sept 2022; revised 2025)CreditTech must operate as a Lending Service Provider (LSP) to a Regulated Entity (RE); the RE, not CreditTech, holds final credit-decision and NPA-recognition responsibility. The 2025 Directions extend coverage to All-India Financial Institutions and formally define Default Loss Guarantee (DLG) arrangements, capped at 5% of the underlying loan portfolio (RBI, 2025; IndiaCorpLaw, 2025).
Default Loss Guarantee (DLG/FLDG) Guidelines (June 2023)Any risk-sharing arrangement between CreditTech and a partner RE must be cash/FD/bank-guarantee backed, capped at 5% of portfolio, and invoked within 120 days of default — CreditTech avoids assuming uncapped credit risk itself.
Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 & DPDP Rules, 2025Consent must be free, specific, informed, unconditional, and revocable; Consent Managers must retain consent records for ≥7 years; data-breach reporting is mandatory to the Data Protection Board of India, in force in phases through May 2027 (MeitY, 2025).
Account Aggregator Master Directions (RBI, 2016, as amended)CreditTech accesses financial data only as a Financial Information User via a licensed NBFC-AA; AAs themselves are barred from reading or storing the data they transmit ("data-blind" design).
Credit Information Companies (Regulation) Act, 2005Any bureau data used must be sourced through a licensed Credit Information Company with borrower consent.
RBI Fair Practices Code & Digital Lending disclosure normsKey Facts Statement (KFS), APR disclosure, and a borrower's right to a look-up/cooling-off period apply to every loan originated through CreditTech-scored applications.

12.2 Privacy-by-design commitments

Data minimisation

Only data with a demonstrable link to a scoring hypothesis (§7) is ingested. Sensitive/protected attributes (caste, religion, health) are never collected; geography and other potential proxy variables are monitored, not blindly used (§7, §3.3).

Purpose limitation & revocability

Every AA consent artifact is scoped to credit-scoring purpose only, time-bound, and revocable at any time by the borrower — consistent with DEPA and DPDP Rules 2025 requirements (Lexology, 2025).

Data localisation & encryption

All borrower data is stored on India-region infrastructure, encrypted at rest and in transit; raw PII is architecturally separated from the model-ready feature store (§8) to limit breach blast-radius.

Independent fairness audit

Given the literature finding that regulatory compliance alone does not eliminate proxy-variable bias (§3.3), CreditTech commits to a periodic third-party fairness audit — approval-rate parity across gender, landholding size, and geography — published to the Institutional Dashboard (M9) and shared with NABARD as pilot sponsor.

Initial 4–5 Village Pilot Design

The pilot is scoped to close the exact research gap identified in §3.5: replicable, village-level, multi-institution evidence across more than one agro-climatic and connectivity context. Site selection below is presented as a criteria-based framework with illustrative representative site-types; final village selection requires ground validation with the NABARD Regional Office and District Development Manager for the chosen state, which is outside the scope of this pre-deployment report.

13.1 Site-selection criteria

Table 13.1 — Selection criteria and rationale
CriterionRationale
Agro-climatic diversityAt least one irrigated/high-KCC-penetration site, one rainfed/climate-exposed site, and one allied-activity site (dairy/fisheries), so the model is validated across cropping and income patterns, not one crop archetype
SHG/FPO densityPreference for blocks with active SHG-BLP or FPO presence, to test the group-data scoring hypothesis (§7) against a real peer-guarantee structure

Existing DPI/lender readinessAt least one site with an existing NABARD/RRB or PACS branch willing to act as the partner Regulated Entity, since CreditTech cannot lend directly (§12)
Baseline data availabilityDigitised or digitisable land records and reasonable satellite/EO coverage, consistent with ULI's own finding that land-record digitisation is the binding constraint on scale-up (Business Standard, 2026)
